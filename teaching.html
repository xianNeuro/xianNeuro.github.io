Teaching

I have taught as a teaching assistant in six courses at two universities, from introductory psychology and statistics to research methods, clinical neuropsychology, and theoretical neuroscience. I teach the way I learned the field: starting from a question a student already cares about, such as why two friends remember the same evening differently, and working backward to the experiment that can settle it. My courses run on three habits. Students argue from data, so every lecture includes a real dataset or figure that the class interprets before I do. Students build something: a stimulus set, an analysis notebook, a pre-registration, a short review. And students explain their work to someone outside the field, because an idea one cannot explain plainly is an idea one does not yet hold.

Courses I am prepared to teach

At the undergraduate level: Introduction to Cognitive Psychology, Research Methods, Cognitive Neuroscience, and Human Memory. I can also teach computational neuroscience at the level of a psychology department's core, and quantitative methods with a modeling emphasis. At the upper-undergraduate and graduate level I would develop two courses that grow out of the lab's program.

  • Naturalistic Cognitive Neuroscience: Stories, Brains, and Language Models. How the field moved from word lists to films and narratives, the analysis methods that came with it (inter-subject correlation, event segmentation, pattern reinstatement), and how language models are now used as raters and as models of comprehension. Students finish with their own analysis of a public naturalistic dataset.
  • Computational Tools for Behavioral Data. Python-based pipelines for transcribing, segmenting, scoring, and modeling rich behavioral data, using the open tools from my lab, so that students leave able to run a naturalistic study end to end.
