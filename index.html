I am a cognitive neuroscientist who studies how memory, meaning, and feeling emerge from experience as it is lived. I am currently a Postdoctoral Researcher in the Department of Psychology at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA), working with Dr. Maureen Ritchey and Dr. Ajay Satpute. My postdoctoral work aims to address how feelings are constructed from memories.


I completed my Ph.D. in Psychological and Brain Sciences at Johns Hopkins University in 2026, co-advised by Dr. Janice Chen and Dr. Christopher Honey . My dissertation, Organization of Real-World Human Memory, built two paradigms for studying naturalistic cognition: choose-your-own-adventure paradigm, which showed that having agency over experiences personalizes memory (Nature Communications, 2026), and interrupted narratives, which revealed a cortical pathway alongside the hippocampal pathway by which the brain keeps a story in mind across interruptions (preprint, under review).


I came to the field of computational cognitive neuroscience through a sequence of labs that together span the methods I use today: brain-network analyses of cognitive reserve in multiple sclerosis patients at the University at Buffalo; auditory perceptual learning, emotional speech, and memory distortion with Dr. Howard Nusbaum and Dr. David Gallo at the University of Chicago (M.A., 2019); and the behavioral and neural signatures of developmental prosopagnosia (face blindness) as a full-time Research Associate with Dr. Joseph DeGutis and Dr. Michael Esterman at Harvard Medical School. That path left me with the question that organizes my work now: how perception, comprehension, memory, and feeling emerge in the flow of more complex everyday lives.


Research interests

I study the emergent properties of naturalistic cognition: how memory, meaning, and feeling arise from temporally extended experience. Three features of real-life experience organize my current work: how does agency change daily cognition, how do we preserve an interrupted thread of thought, how do feelings emerge from memory-based situation comprehension. Each line of my work opens the next: choosing what happens next personalizes memory organization; an organized memory must be preserved across interruptions in a world full of distractions; the memory preserved should shape how new events feel. The last is the question of my postdoctoral work.


Findings so far, and the instrument behind them

  • Choice makes memory ours. In choose-your-own-adventure stories read by 324 participants, having agency did not simply strengthen memory; it personalized it, moving each reader's recall away from the generic semantic space that a model of everyone's text would predict and toward the individual. Nature Communications, 2026.
  • The story persists in an inverted code. In 73 adults listening to interrupted stories, two pathways kept the narrative alive: hippocampal activity at each interruption that predicted later recall, and a posterior medial cortex pattern that persisted through pauses and a competing task in an inverted code, predicting restoration of default mode activity and memory beyond the hippocampal effect. Preprint, 2026, under review; data and code.
  • Spontaneous thought moves through the same networks. Transitions in the free flow of thought, spoken aloud in the scanner, recruit the default and control networks the way external event boundaries do. Nature Communications, 2025 (second author); annotated dataset in Scientific Data, 2026.
  • People and language models as subjects of the same science. I built narRaters (GitHub), an open human-in-the-loop pipeline that scores narrative recall and rates narrative structure with local or cloud models, and I measure how closely models agree with people on the causal structure of stories and on what is remembered (Conference on Cognitive Computational Neuroscience, 2026; manuscript in preparation).
