Research

How do meaning and feeling emerge from the experiences we live through?

Lived experience is not a sequence of isolated trials. It unfolds continuously, accumulates over a lifetime, and is shaped by our choices and punctuated by interruptions. I study the emergent properties of naturalistic cognition: the memories, interpretations, and feelings that arise when a mind with a history meets events as they unfold. Memory research increasingly uses stories and films, yet many paradigms still overlook three defining features of lived experience: we actively shape its course, we continually navigate internal and external interruptions, and our feelings reflect both the present moment and our past. My research brings these features together to understand how cognitive and neural processes support everyday life.


The three branches below build on one another. Choosing what happens next personalizes memory, suggesting that memory functions as more than a record: it is a scaffold built around the person. As events unfold, that scaffold must survive the interruptions of everyday life, raising the question of how the brain preserves it against interference. That same scaffold may also shape how new events feel—a question at the heart of my postdoctoral work. I combine naturalistic experiments in which people read, listen to, or choose their way through stories with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and large language models (LLMs) to study how experience takes shape over time. Understanding memory as a constructed, personal scaffold also has implications for AI: it helps clarify what systems that read, remember, and personalize should emulate about human cognition, and what they may not capture on their own.

1. Agency: how acting on events shapes what we remember

In real life we are not passive recipients of events; we choose, and our choices change what happens next. This branch asks how that sense of agency changes memory for the events themselves.

Choose your own adventure: when readers steer the story, their memories become their own

To study agency in a naturalistic setting we built the choose-your-own-adventure (CYOA) paradigm: a branching story presented a few sentences at a time, in which readers choose what happens next. Comparing readers who had full, partial, or no control over the path of the same story, we found that agency personalizes episodic memory: readers who chose recalled more idiosyncratic combinations of the same events, and their memories were less predictable from generic sentence embeddings, rather than simply better or worse. Choice did not add memory; it moved memory away from the space that a model of everyone's text would predict, and toward the person. That makes memory look less like a record and more like a scaffold built around the individual, and it raises the question of the next branch: how is that scaffold held when experience is interrupted?

2. Continuity: how the mind keeps a thread through interruptions and wandering thoughts

A memory scaffold is built while experience unfolds, yet experience is interrupted all the time, by pauses, by other people, by our own thoughts, and we pick the story back up. This branch asks how the brain keeps prior mental context available across interruptions, and what happens when thought runs freely.

Where was I? How the brain keeps a story alive across interruptions

Seventy-three adults listened to fourteen-minute stories interrupted after every forty seconds by silent pauses or by a competing theory-of-mind task, or heard scrambled controls. Using fMRI, we asked how the brain keeps prior mental context available across interruptions. Two pathways emerged. Hippocampal activity rose at each interruption onset, and the size of that rise predicted later recall, a signature of indexing the epoch just ended. Independently, posterior medial cortex carried the interrupted narrative as a reliable, gradually evolving pattern, an "episodic background" that persisted through the pauses and even through the competing task. The persisting cortical pattern was not a copy of the story pattern that preceded it: it was inverted, and the degree of inversion predicted how well it persisted. If a many-minute mental context is protected by transformation rather than by rehearsal, then continuity is a representational format, not an act of will.
preprint (bioRxiv, 2026) data and code CCN 2025 abstract

Thinking out loud: how memories and plans surface in the free flow of thought

Interruptions also come from within. Participants verbalized their thoughts continuously in the scanner. With Dr. Hongmi Lee and colleagues, we characterized the neural dynamics of spontaneous memory recall and future thinking in that continuous flow of thought, and released the annotated dataset. Transitions in spontaneous thought recruit the default and control networks the way external event boundaries do, which is what lets the interruption work above be extended to interruptions that people generate themselves. Together, the first two branches describe a structured, person-specific representation that is built while events unfold and held across interruption. The third asks how it is reused to construct emotion and social meaning.
paper (Nature Communications, 2025) dataset paper (Scientific Data, 2026)

3. Feeling: how memory builds social and emotional experience

If memory is a scaffold rather than a record, it should shape how new events feel. Constructionist and predictive theories of emotion make exactly that claim, yet direct tests are almost absent, because in ordinary stimuli the emotion of a scene cannot be separated from the scene. This is the focus of my postdoctoral work at UCLA with Dr. Maureen Ritchey and Dr. Ajay Satpute, which began in October 2026.

The instrument: humans and LLMs as subjects of the same science

The three branches share a method that did not exist five years ago. LLMs can annotate narrative experience at scale, and they can be studied as a second kind of comprehender. Where models and people agree, naturalistic memory research gains tools that scale by an order of magnitude; where they diverge, the divergence maps what human comprehension adds.

The causal backbone of a story: what holds events together, as judged by people and by language models

A long story is not a list of events but a web of causes and consequences, and that web may be what memory holds on to. I measure how closely language models agree with people on the causal structure of stories, and whether model-derived causal centrality predicts human recall as well as human-derived centrality does. Treating the model as a rememberer, I also compare the organization of its recall (compression, ordering, causal clustering, temporal contiguity) with the human distribution. Presented at the Conference on Cognitive Computational Neuroscience (CCN) 2026 (Li, Li & Chen); manuscript in preparation.

narRaters: open-source narrative recall processing, with a web interface at every step

Rich narrative data have always been the field's bottleneck: scoring a ten-minute story's free recall means hundreds of clause-to-event judgments per participant, and rating the causal relations among events grows with the square of story length. narRaters (narrative + raters) is an open-source pipeline and web interface that transcribes recall, segments stories into events, matches recall to events, and rates causal relations, using rule-based methods, local models, or cloud models, with a human review screen after every automated step. It is public on GitHub and PyPI with an archived release: python3 -m pip install narraters, then narraters serve.

Related and earlier work

The methods above were built in projects on perception and brain networks that preceded or ran alongside the three branches.

Each viewer's own path: how self-guided exploration of a scene shapes the brain's response to it

Studies of scene perception often use brief presentations of under a second, yet when people freely explore a complex photograph or painting, each person follows their own "exploration path" through the scene, and the history of that path may shape how they understand the next moment. Using fMRI, we tested whether exploration paths predict neural response patterns in the default mode network (DMN), both within and across people. Presented at the Society for Neuroscience (SfN) 2021 (talk and poster).

Foundations: faces, attention, and brain networks

Before the Ph.D. I worked on the behavioral and neural signatures of developmental prosopagnosia (face blindness) with Dr. Joseph DeGutis and Dr. Michael Esterman at Harvard Medical School, on reward and visual attention with event-related potentials (ERPs) and fMRI at Capital Normal University, and on functional and structural brain networks in multiple sclerosis at the University at Buffalo.
